An atomic force microscope that runs by itself

A design, a controller proven in simulation, and a step-by-step tutorial for an AFM built from cheap, easy-to-buy parts. The control software is written in cancho; the FPGA logic is generated from it.

Read the tutorial What is measured Source
Design stage. Nothing is built, nothing is measured on hardware, no part has been bought. Every number on this page is either from a simulation in this repository (marked simulated) or a plan (marked plan). The tutorial's steps have not been carried out by anyone yet, and each says so.

What it is

An AFM feels a surface with a very sharp tip and draws it. A feedback loop keeps the tip at a fixed distance by moving it up and down; that up-and-down record is the picture. Here the sensor is a quartz tuning fork with a tungsten tip. Nearer the surface the fork vibrates less, and the controller holds that amplitude constant.

1. Approach

Bring the tip to the surface without crashing it, with no one at the knobs. A limit and a deadman in hardware stop the Z drive whatever the software asks.

2. Tune

Find the fork's resonance, choose drive, setpoint and gains, and print what it chose. The least certain step: how well this works on a real fork is unknown.

3. Scan

Raster the requested area; if the loop saturates, slow down or stop; if Z hits its limit, retract.

4. Report

Write the image with every setting and every automatic decision, in a plain file, so a run can be repeated.

The rules this project follows

  • Cheap, easy to buy. A development board, a tuning fork, tungsten wire, piezo disks, a stepper; AFM cantilevers (about USD 21 each) for the faster second stage. No ready-made AFM, no lab controller.
  • What we make is open. Software and generated gateware under EUPL-1.2, hardware under CERN-OHL-S-2.0, docs under CC BY 4.0 proposed, to be confirmed.
  • Claims are measured or labelled. A result that turns out false is corrected where it was made.

What the simulation also showed

  • The square-wave amplitude detector gives the same figure at every signal phase when the drive and the detector share one oscillator, and noise of about 17% of the signal moves it under 2%.
  • A weakness: a third harmonic at 20% of the signal raises the figure about 7%. Fix: a band-pass before the ADC, or sine references.
  • Two lessons from tuning: the loop's first 8-bit gain fraction was too coarse for a slow tuning fork, so the host shifts the detector figure down first; and an integral term made the loop swing between its stops until it was made slower than the plant. With 16-bit gains the integral can be that slow, and the loop now has one.
  • The Z drive needs a 16-bit DAC. The 2025 paper's 12-bit DAC gave 0.24 nm steps and read a 0.34 nm atomic step as 0.48 nm.

What the simulator showed before any hardware existed

  • The reference's 16-bit phase could not resolve a fork's 6.5 Hz-wide resonance (a 4 Hz step): it is 32 bits now.
  • A scan advanced a pixel per loop step, which smears features the loop needs about 20 steps to follow: a pixel divider was added.
  • With the first, coarser gain format the loop could only be proportional, and a 100 pm grating came out at 40 to 60% of its height; with 16-bit gains it can have a slow integral term, and the same scan gives about 89% at 60 loop steps per pixel (and a slight overshoot at 120). The 16 pm DAC step still sets the floor.
  • The board has no coarse-motor interface yet, and a pixel-synchronous read or a small capture memory will be needed on a real board.

These are predictions of this software; details and how far to trust them: docs/simulator.md.

Not done

The converters and Pmods attached to the board, the amplifier built, a motor turning, a run on the instrument, the host program and sweep driver, a register contract for the host code, any real sensor, scanner, amplifier or mechanics, and every step of the hardware tutorial below.

How it is put together

Three layers, and one rule: the part that can damage the instrument sits behind a limit and a deadman that live in hardware, so a software fault cannot drive the tip into the sample.

Why generated, and what that buys

One graph per block gives widths from declared input ranges, registers placed automatically, and a test that compares the Verilog (under Icarus) to the cancho evaluation on every corner of the input ranges. A second, hand-written cancho model of each block is kept as an independent cross-check.

Tutorial: build it

For someone with basic electronics and no AFM experience. Parts 0 to 2 can be done today and have been. Parts 3 to 12 are a plan (their control logic is simulated where noted): each ends with a Check (a measurement and a pass criterion written before the step) and says "not done by anyone yet". If a Check fails, stop and write down what you saw, with numbers.

Read this first. This is a draft written before anything was built. It is not a safety assessment. Do not build the amplifier from this page: its design is not written. Do not leave an instrument powered and unattended until Part 10's tests have passed.
PART 0

What you are building

A quartz tuning fork (the 32.768 kHz crystal in a watch) has a tungsten tip glued to one prong. A small drive makes it vibrate. As the tip nears a surface the vibration amplitude falls. A controller moves the sample up and down to keep that amplitude at a fixed fraction of its free value; the Z positions it records, point by point as a second motion moves the sample sideways, are the image.

The vibration is measured by a lock-in: the ADC signal is multiplied by square waves at the drive frequency, 90 degrees apart, summed over a window, and the amplitude is the square root of the two sums squared. That is what sim/lockin.cho does.

Check. You can say in your own words what the loop holds constant, and what the picture is.

Not done by anyone yet.

PART 1

Safety and the bench

Hazards of this kind of instrument, in general.
  • Chemicals: tungsten tips are etched in a sodium-hydroxide bath with a small voltage. Eye protection and gloves; follow chemical-safety practice for your jurisdiction.
  • Voltage: piezo disks driven from a DAC and a current amplifier work at about 10 V in the 2025 paper's build (their figure). Anything higher, such as a piezo tube, needs a fused, enclosed supply and is not covered here.
  • Static: the FPGA board and converters; use a wrist strap.
  • The tip: contact at speed destroys it. The hardware limit exists for this and is tested before a tip is mounted.

Bench: a multimeter, soldering station, fine tweezers, a 10x to 30x loupe or stereo microscope, and an oscilloscope if you can; a heavy base on soft support for vibration isolation (its design is an open question).

Check. You have read the list above and know where the power switch is.

Not done by anyone yet.

PART 2

Run the controller in simulation works today

No hardware. You need the cancho compiler, Icarus Verilog and Yosys.

git clone https://github.com/alpibrusl/cancho-afm
cd cancho-afm

# the model of tip, surface and loop
cancho test tests/loop_test.cho sim/loop.cho --std

# the PID, the raster and the detector against their generated hardware
brew install icarus-verilog yosys      # or your package manager
sh gen/check.sh                        # exits 0 when every comparison passes
  1. Open sim/loop.cho: the tip-and-surface model and the one-line control step.
  2. Change the gain shift in a test and watch how capture time and tracking lag change.
  3. Run the whole automatic procedure against the software twin, with no hardware: cancho run host/demo_twin.cho ... (the command is in docs/simulator.md). It prints what it found, what it chose and the scanned image. sh test_all.sh runs every test in the project.
  4. Open gen/pid_ir.cho: the PID written once as a graph. Change a limit and rerun gen/check.sh.
Check. All the tests pass; you can explain why a stronger gain captures faster but can ring. (The runs made while writing this page, 2026-11-01, passed; the explanation is yours.)

PART 4

Bring up the FPGA board

  1. Install the open-source flow: Yosys, nextpnr (the Gowin build, nextpnr-himbaechel), Apycula, openFPGALoader. The YosysHQ OSS CAD Suite ships all four, including for Raspberry Pi (arm64).
  2. Build and load cancho-hdl's register-bridge design for the Tang Nano 20K (see that project's boards/tang-nano-20k/). Check each pin in its constraint file against Sipeed's schematic first: the file itself says the numbers are from memory.
  3. Read a register from your computer over the board's serial port, with a cancho program.
If the board is shared with another project, loading a design replaces what is on it. Check first.
Check. You write a value to a register and read the same value back, and you keep the bitstream's build log showing the timing it reports against the 27 MHz clock.

Done once, on 2026-11-02: the full controller (with the motor driver) was built on a Raspberry Pi and loaded into a Tang Nano 20K, and 19 of 19 checks over its real serial port passed: the identifier reads back, every limit and the motor start closed, read-back works, writes to read-only and unmapped registers are refused, a motor command is refused while MOTOR_MAX is zero, and 50 reads in a row stay in step. This confirms the clock, serial pins and baud rate. Nothing wired to the other pins (the Pmods, the motor driver) has been tried. Details: docs/first-hardware.md.

PART 5

Converters

The board has no ADC or DAC. The requirements, derived in docs/converters.md from stated assumptions:

  • ADC: at least 8 samples per fork period: 262 kS/s for a 32.768 kHz fork. 12 bits is enough (the window averaging adds about 3.5 bits); 16 gives margin. The detector's sums must stay within +-8,388,607, so window x full-scale count must stay below that.
  • DAC: 16 bits or better, four channels (X, Y, Z, drive).

Candidates: one Pmod AD1 and four Pmod DA3 (Digilent), in stock at DigiKey France at about EUR 189 together with VAT; other European shops show them withdrawn. See docs/converter-candidates.md for what is confirmed and what must be read before buying. Wire them to the FPGA per their datasheets, with no tip and no scanner attached.

Check. Write a ramp on the DAC, read it with the ADC, and write down the error between them. It must be within the converters' stated accuracy.

Not done on hardware by anyone. The SPI masters for the two candidate modules are written and pass in simulation; the glue to the detector and loop is not.

PART 6

Read the tuning fork

  1. Mount a fork on a small board (the leads are fragile; open the can with care).
  2. Drive it with a small sine from the DAC near 32.768 kHz, and turn its current into a voltage with a transimpedance amplifier into the ADC.
  3. Sweep the drive frequency, run the detector at each step, and plot the amplitude figure against frequency.

Drive and detector share one oscillator by design, so the reference always matches the drive. A reference 0.5% off over a 32-period window costs about 7% (simulated).

Check. A sharp peak near 32.768 kHz. Write down its frequency and width; the width gives the fork's Q and so how fast the amplitude can respond (about Q divided by pi times f0; for a Q of several thousand that is roughly a tenth of a second or less).

Not done by anyone yet. The sweep and resonance finder are not written.

PART 7

Make a tip

  1. Etch tungsten wire to a point in a sodium-hydroxide bath with a small voltage. The hobbyist build this route follows is described at hackaday.io/project/206429; copy its recipe, and record the values you actually use.
  2. Look at the tip under the loupe: a smooth point and no bend.
  3. Glue it to one prong. Re-measure the resonance (Part 6).
Check. The resonance is still there; write down by how much its frequency and its width changed.

Not done by anyone yet. That source is a self-reported hobbyist project, not verified here.

PART 8

Scanner and its amplifier

The scanner moves the sample in X and Y and the tip relative to it in Z. The plan is piezo disks (the "buzzers" in beepers), which the 2025 paper drove at about +-10 V: five disks, two pairs for X and Y and one for Z, on long legs for range. That removes the high-voltage amplifier. The amplifier between a DAC channel and a disk is then a small current-capable op-amp stage.

The amplifier is designed (in a circuit simulator, not built): a gain of 7.98 with a reference offset for X and Y (DAC 0 to 2.5 V becomes -10 to +10 V), a gain of 4.01 for Z (0 to +10 V), and for the fork a 1/50.9 drive attenuator, a 1 Mohm transimpedance stage with a trimmer that cancels the fork's parallel capacitance, and an AC-coupled gain stage centred on the AD1's 1.65 V. A simulation of the piezo drivers with the load capacitor and the op-amp's output resistance swept forced a change: the isolation resistor alone left a 2.4 V overshoot with a plausible output resistance, so the design takes its DC feedback from the load side and closes the loop at high frequency through a small capacitor from the op-amp's own output. Full design, results, parts and prices (about EUR 55 to 80): docs/amplifier.md.

Still open: the piezo disk's capacitance and voltage rating (assumed 30 nF; the design holds to about 100 nF), the fork's real C0, Q and motional resistance, the op-amp's vendor model, and the supply's current budget. The scanner legs, the frame and the disks' mounting are not designed.
Check. With a dummy 30 nF capacitor instead of the piezo: 0 V out at mid-code (after trimming the reference), 9.97 V at full scale on X/Y, 10 V on Z, and a step with no overshoot; then a known DAC step moves the sample by a distance you measure against the grating (Part 11).

Designed and simulated; not built.

PART 9

Mechanics and the coarse approach

  • Frame: rigid, symmetric (to cancel drift). The 2025 paper used 11 pieces of circuit-board material and reported 6.2 nm/min drift (their number); our frame is ours to design, and nothing is drawn.
  • Approach: a stepper and fine-pitch screw bring tip and surface from millimetres to within the Z piezo's range, in small steps with the Z scanner extended and retracted between them. The motor and screw are not chosen. The board now has the interface: STEP, DIR and ENABLE pins for an A4988-style driver, with a command of N signed microsteps, a rate, and a maximum number of steps per command that is zero after power-on, so nothing moves until the host allows it; it stops when disabled or when the deadman latches. Tested in simulation and in the generated hardware's simulation, not yet with a motor.
  • Vibration: a heavy base on soft support; the design is an open question.
Check. With the tip well away, drive the stepper by the computer through its full travel in both directions and back, with nothing touching.

Not done by anyone yet.

PART 10

First power-up: limit and deadman before any tip meets any surface

Do not skip. These tests are the reason the tip survives.
  1. Limit: set a Z limit in the FPGA, ask the host for a value past it, read the output on a meter. It must stay at the limit. (Simulated: the output clamps to the host's limit whatever the PID asks.)
  2. Deadman: start the loop, stop the host program, and watch the Z output go to the retract value within the timeout and stay there until reset. (Simulated: fault latches, Z goes to the clamped retract value, new samples are refused.)
  3. Host reads back the limit it set and refuses to run if it does not match.
Check. Both behaviours seen on a meter or scope, each with the number you read written down.

Behaviour simulated; not done on hardware by anyone. The read-back register contract is not written.

PART 11

Tune, approach, and the first image

  1. Tune: sweep, find the peak, set the drive, take the free amplitude, set the setpoint at a fraction of it, set the gains. The program prints what it chose.
  2. Approach: step the motor, extend Z, look for the amplitude to fall to the setpoint, retract and repeat; stop at the first contact. You watch, a hand on the power switch.
  3. Scan a calibration grating (a stated period, for example a few micrometres), large and slow. The raster is a triangle: npix points out, back, then the next line; the slow axis steps at the end of each line.
Check. The grating's period measured from the image agrees with its stated period; write down the difference and its sign.

Not done on hardware by anyone. The tune and approach logic is written (sim/tune.cho, sim/approach.cho) and passes against a model, including the end-to-end chain; the program that drives the sweep through the FPGA is not written.

PART 12

Unattended runs and the record

Start a scan from the interface and leave it to the program: it approaches, tunes, scans, retracts on any fault (lost signal, timeout, limit hit, host quiet) and writes the image with every setting and every automatic decision in a plain file. Repeat a run from its record.

Check. A second run made from the first run's record gives an image whose grating period agrees with the first to within the noise you measured in Part 11.

Not done by anyone yet.

WHEN IT GOES WRONG
Retract first (the deadman does it on its own if the host goes quiet). Then write down what you saw, with numbers, in an issue on the repository. Do not push on after a failed Check.

Is a high-speed AFM possible?

Yes in principle for what the electronics here do, but not with the tuning fork, and not yet with this parts rule. This is what the published DIY work says and what our numbers show; none of it is measured by this project.

What limits an AFM's speed

  1. The cantilever's response. Roughly tau = Q / (pi f0). A tuning fork (f0 = 32.768 kHz, Q of several thousand in air, an assumption) responds in roughly 0.05 to 0.1 seconds: an amplitude-mode fork AFM is for slow scans, whatever the electronics. A small cantilever (f0 near 1 MHz, Q about 100, an assumption) responds in tens of microseconds.
  2. The Z scanner. A piezo disk or stack responds in the kilohertz range (assumption); a fast scan with feedback needs a small, stiff, fast Z actuator.
  3. The XY scanner's resonances. The 2025 paper's buzzer scanner stayed clean to 40 lines/s with a sinusoidal fast axis; its range grew at 60 and 80 lines/s, which they attribute to resonance (their report).
  4. The electronics. Here: the detector takes one sample per clock, so at 27 MHz it keeps up with any ADC up to that rate; the Z loop does 6.75 M samples/s; the raster is one tick per sample. These are simulation and timing estimates.

What others have published

WorkSpeed (self-reported)How
Wang et al., 2025, DIY AFM on PCB frame40 lines/s clean; 6.2 nm/min driftoptical pickup sensor, piezo disk scanner, sine fast axis; the speed test was at constant height, with a soft cantilever and no Z feedback
Liao et al., HardwareX 2022, open controller55 lines/s; 512 x 512 in 9.3 sconstant-height mode on a commercial simplified AFM, a closed NI controller

Both fast numbers are constant-height imaging: the Z loop is not running, so the speed is a property of the scanner and the sample being gentle. Feedback imaging at high speed needs small, fast cantilevers and a fast Z actuator.

A staged path

  1. Slow and automatic, tuning fork. Parts 0 to 12. The goal is a correct image of a grating.
  2. Fast X and Y. Write the sinusoidal fast axis (not written), measure the scanner's response and the line rate at which the image stays true. Needs only the scanner, no new cantilever.
  3. Constant-height fast imaging of a rough sample such as a disc's tracks: a soft cantilever is needed, which is one purchased part; cantilevers are allowed (the owner, 2026-11-01), together with a deflection sensor: a salvaged DVD optical pickup or a laser and photodiode.
  4. Feedback at speed. A small cantilever (hundreds of kHz to MHz), an ADC of at least 2.4 to 8 MS/s, and a fast Z actuator. The generated detector and loop would be reused unchanged except for the ADC rate and window; the actuator is the open problem. This is slice S8 of the design and has no result.

Honest summary: the controller is not the obstacle; the sensor and the Z actuator are. Whether an all-cheap, all-open build can go fast is unknown, and S8's acceptance is a measured line rate on a small fast cantilever.
